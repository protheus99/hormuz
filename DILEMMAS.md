# Ethical dilemmas — the deck

Twenty-eight scenarios and six escapes for §12A.6, drafted 2026-09-22 for the owner to cut, keep and
add to. None is built yet.

## The rule these are written under

**No dilemma may be a storyline from a television programme, or close to one.** The owner suggested
*Landman* as a well of examples and then, rightly, drew the line: thematic debt is fine, borrowed
plot is not. An earlier draft drifted over it — a widow pressed to settle before finding counsel, a
crew chief without the right to work, a seventeen-year-old who lied about his age — and those are
gone.

What replaced them keeps the rule by construction: **every dilemma arises from a system this game
already has.** Leases and their reserves, the sealed auction, operating licences, chokepoints and
war-risk cover, chartered tankers, cargo at sea, fixed-price deals, the credit line, the maintenance
and workover machinery. A situation that can only exist in a game with a Strait of Hormuz and a
sealed lease round cannot be anybody else's plot, and it is *playable* rather than merely dramatic.

Generic hazards of the industry — a flare over its permit, a spill nobody wants to report — stay,
because those belong to the industry and its regulators rather than to any programme about it.

## The rules they all follow (§12A.6)

- **No meters.** Four numbers would decide the matter. These state what is certain and say plainly
  that the rest cannot be projected.
- **The payoff is Enron-shaped.** Cutting the corner usually pays, sometimes for years. What builds
  is hidden exposure, which never washes off.
- **The reckoning takes what the corner was protecting**, not money. Measured: $1M of exposure
  carried three years costs 1.3% of what a producer earns, and a third of the time nothing at all. A
  fine is a line item at any size. So **every entry names what it risks**, and the reckoning reads it.
- **It follows trouble** — likelier when cash is low, prices have fallen and a deadline is near.
- **The pressure is hinted at.** Alerts and news, never numbers: an insurer asking for maintenance
  records, a regulator wanting three years of flaring returns, word that a file has been opened.
- **There is a way out, at a price** — see *The escapes* below. The hints are the window.
- **Told plainly, and rare.** A deck this size is a career's worth, not a year's.

*Certain* is what the card may state as fact. *Hidden* is what the engine decides in secret and the
player may never learn. *Risks* is what a reckoning would take.

## A. The wells and the crews on them
*Built on the maintenance interval, the service hold, the workover queue and the rising hazard.*

**1. The service you can postpone** · producer
Prices are the best they have been all year and four wells are due to be pulled for a service.
*Yes* pull them now · *Maybe* two now, two next month · *No* hold the services until prices turn.
**Certain:** the downtime at today's price. **Hidden:** what the hazard does while you wait.
**Risks:** that lease, shut. **Uses:** the service hold, directly.

**2. The manager's hunch** · producer
A maintenance manager wants wells pulled early. He cannot say why, only that he does not like what he
is seeing.
*Yes* pull them · *Maybe* the two he is most worried about · *No* carry on.
**Hidden:** whether he was right at all — decided in secret, unconnected to the wells' true state, so
the board cannot be read for the answer. **Risks:** a well lost, and the lease shut while it is
looked into.

**3. The crew that has been on shift** · producer
Wells are down and a crew can be paid to come straight from another job to get them back this week.
*Yes* bring them now · *Maybe* the two worst wells · *No* wait for a rested crew.
**Risks:** a well lost. **Uses:** the rush lever.

**4. The detectors** · producer, refiner
A third of the gas detectors on site fail their test. A full replacement is expensive.
*Yes* replace them all · *Maybe* the worst of them · *No* recalibrate and carry on.
**Hidden:** whether the ones you skipped were the ones that mattered. **Risks:** the site shut.

**5. The inspection you were warned about** · producer, refiner
You hear inspectors are coming in ten days.
*Yes* fix what is wrong · *Maybe* fix what they will look at · *No* make the site presentable.
**Risks:** the operating licence for that region.

**6. The cheaper contractor** · all
A contractor undercuts the others by a third. Everybody knows why: how he runs his crews.
*Yes* pay the going rate · *Maybe* hire him and audit him · *No* take the saving.
**Risks:** the site shut; partners slower to deal.

## B. The ground, the water and the air
*Built on leases, their reserves, and what a block comes with.*

**7. The creek** · producer
A leak has reached water at the edge of the lease. It is small.
*Yes* report and clean it · *Maybe* clean it quietly · *No* wait for the rain.
**Certain:** reporting means a shutdown. **Risks:** the lease shut for a clean-up you no longer choose
the timing of.

**8. The flare** · producer
You are flaring above what the permit allows. Fixing it means shutting in for a fortnight.
*Yes* shut in and fix it · *Maybe* cut the rate to what is legal · *No* keep flaring.
**Risks:** the permit, and with it the lease.

**9. The wells that came with the block** · producer
The ground you took at auction has eleven old wells on it, unplugged, that nobody has been
responsible for in thirty years.
*Yes* plug them all · *Maybe* the two that are leaking · *No* they were there before you.
**Risks:** the block forfeited — you took the ground, you took what was on it. **Uses:** the
abandonment liability deferred from §12A.8.

**10. The disposal well** · producer
Your produced water goes down a disposal well taking more than its permit allows. The alternative is
trucking it, at a price.
*Yes* truck it · *Maybe* cut injection to the permitted rate and lose output · *No* carry on.
**Risks:** the permit, then the lease.

**11. The neighbour's water** · producer
The landowner next to your block says your water use has drawn his well down. He may be right; the
hydrology is genuinely unclear.
*Yes* pay what he asks · *Maybe* a contribution and a new pump · *No* let him prove it.
**Risks:** a judgment against the block itself.

**12. The road** · producer, trader
Your trucks have broken up the only road through a village. The council has written twice.
*Yes* rebuild it · *Maybe* pay the council to · *No* run at night.
**Risks:** the licence to operate in that region.

## C. Cargo, ships and straits
*Built on chokepoints, war-risk cover, charters, demurrage and cargo at sea. None of this exists
outside a game with a map like ours.*

**13. The strait your insurer has excluded** · trader, refiner
Cover has been withdrawn for the strait your cargo is booked through. The long way round costs two
weeks and a great deal of money.
*Yes* go the long way · *Maybe* hold the cargo and wait · *No* sail it uninsured.
**Certain:** the cost of the detour. **Hidden:** whether anything happens in the strait this week.
**Risks:** the cargo, entire, and cover withdrawn for good.

**14. The cheap ship** · trader, refiner
A charter is offered well under the market. The vessel's inspection record explains why.
*Yes* take a sound ship · *No* take the rate.
**Risks:** the cargo, and the ship detained with it aboard.

**15. Washing tanks at sea** · trader
Cleaning tanks properly at a reception facility costs money and a day. There is another way, and it
is a long way from anywhere.
*Yes* pay for the facility · *No* wash at sea.
**Risks:** the right to call at that region's ports.

**16. The cargo whose papers do not agree** · trader, refiner
A cargo is offered under the market. Its papers say one origin; its history suggests another you are
not allowed to buy from.
*Yes* refuse it · *Maybe* demand the chain of title · *No* take it and ask nothing.
**Risks:** the cargo seized, and a ban on trading in that region.

**17. Two per cent short** · trader
The buyer's surveyor is not thorough, and your cargo is short.
*Yes* tell him · *No* let it go.
**Risks:** the deal cancelled, and counterparties who will not sign the next one.

**18. The delivery date you cannot make** · trader, producer
A strait is closing and your buyer's cargo will be late. Telling them now lets them buy elsewhere.
*Yes* tell them today · *Maybe* tell them when you are sure · *No* let it arrive when it arrives.
**Risks:** the deal cancelled against you, penalty included.

**19. The demurrage claim** · trader
Your ship waited six days. The paperwork would support nine.
*Yes* claim six · *No* claim nine.
**Risks:** the claim clawed back, and that counterparty closed to you.

**20. The berth** · trader
Your cargo is fourth in the queue. A payment to the right official makes it first.
*Yes* wait your turn · *No* pay.
**Risks:** the right to call at that port.

## D. The books, the market and the auction
*Built on the credit line, the sealed auction, licences and campaign goals.*

**21. The reserves report** · producer
Your engineer's estimate for a block is well below the figure you published last year. The bank lends
against the published figure.
*Yes* publish his number · *Maybe* publish a range · *No* republish last year's.
**Risks:** the credit line, withdrawn in full. **Mechanical:** the line follows the published figure,
so the lie pays the same week.

**22. The survey that was not shot for you** · producer
A contractor who shot a seismic survey for a rival will sell you a copy before the sale.
*Yes* buy it · *No* bid on the published survey like everyone else.
**Risks:** the block it won you, forfeited with the oil still under it. **Mechanical:** reveals a
lot's true band. The strongest fit in the deck.

**23. The number you were not meant to hear** · producer
The night before the sale, someone tells you what a rival means to bid.
*Yes* use it · *No* bid as you were going to.
**Risks:** the block, and exclusion from the next round.

**24. A friend at the ministry** · producer
A licence in a region that requires one could be had in weeks rather than a year, for a consultancy
fee to the right firm.
*Yes* pay it · *No* wait your turn.
**Risks:** that licence, revoked, and no second application.

**25. Holding barrels back** · producer, trader
The market for your grade is thin this month. Holding your barrels off it for a fortnight would move
the price your way — and everybody else's.
*Yes* sell as usual · *No* hold them back.
**Risks:** a forced sale at the regulator's price, and the region closed to you.

**26. The informant** · all
Someone inside a rival offers to tell you what they are doing, for a price.
*Yes* pay · *No* decline.
**Hidden:** whether the information is any good, and whether the rival finds out.
**Risks:** a judgment, and that rival refusing every deal thereafter.

**27. The counterparty you know is finished** · trader, refiner
A company wants a ninety-day deal. You have good reason to think it will not last sixty, and the
cancellation penalty would be yours to collect.
*Yes* decline the deal · *Maybe* offer thirty days · *No* sign the ninety.
**Risks:** the penalty clawed back, and partners who have heard about it.

**28. The quarter that nearly worked** · all
You are a fortnight from the end and just short of the number. A cargo still at sea could be booked
as sold today.
*Yes* book it when it lands · *No* book it now.
**Risks:** the result itself — a scenario won this way can be taken back.
**Mechanical:** only appears when a campaign goal is genuinely within reach, which is the only time
the temptation is real.

## The escapes

*The owner's addition, 2026-09-22: a player should be able to get out from under it before it lands
— at a price.* This is what companies actually do, and it makes the hints worth reading rather than
merely ominous: a hint is the window, and the window closes.

**Three rules that keep an escape from being a free pass:**

1. **It costs a multiple of what the corner saved, not a margin over it.** A player who cuts corners
   *planning* to clean up keeps the saving every time and pays only when a hint reaches them, so the
   escape has to cost more than `saving ÷ hint rate` before that plan even breaks even — at a 60–70%
   hint rate, more than 1.7×. The prices below are set well above that:

   | Taken | Costs | Why |
   |---|---|---|
   | Before any hint | 1.2× the saving | Nobody does this, and it should be cheap if they do |
   | At the first hint | 2.5× | The real decision, and already a bad bargain |
   | At the last hint, file opened | 5×, and counsel only | Softens what is taken; nothing more is on offer |

   At a 65% hint rate the wait-and-clean plan therefore costs about 2.3× what it saved, against 1×
   for simply doing the job properly. It loses, and it loses obviously.
2. **It gets dearer the longer it is left.** Early and cheap, late and ruinous. That is the whole
   decision.
3. **Something always remains.** An escape converts a reckoning in kind into a cost in money and a
   residue of about a fifth on the record. It never returns you to clean — "there should always be a
   cost".

**E1. Put it right** · removes the entry it addresses
Plug the wells you left, service the field you held back, clean the creek properly. Costs money and
downtime; the cheapest escape if taken while the thing is still small.

**E2. Tell them first** · converts in kind to cash
Voluntary disclosure. A known penalty and a shutdown you choose the timing of, in exchange for the
block, the licence or the cargo staying yours. Only available before a file is opened — the second
hint is the last moment it is offered.

**E3. Settle it** · removes one entry, adds a smaller one
Pay the landowner, the buyer, the council. It goes away, and paying to make something go away is
itself a corner: a little goes back on the record.

**E4. Put a compliance programme in** · standing cost, lowers the odds
A monthly cost that halves the chance of a reckoning while it runs and slowly retires the oldest
entries. Dull, expensive, and the only thing that works on a whole career rather than one corner.

**E5. Insure against it** · covers the money, not the thing
A premium now. A reckoning's cash penalty is met, but nothing insures a forfeited block or a revoked
licence, which is the part that hurts.

**E6. Retain counsel** · softens what is taken
Once a file is opened, lawyers can usually turn a forfeiture into a shutdown, or a revocation into a
suspension. Expensive, and it does not make the file go away.

## What is built (2026-09-23)

**Six cards:** **1** the service you can postpone · **2** the manager's hunch · **9** the wells that
came with the block · **21** the reserves report · **22** the survey that was not shot for you ·
**23** the number you were not meant to hear. **24** the ministry waits for stage 3b, since a licence
in a region a producer cannot work is worth nothing to buy.

**All three escapes** — E1 put it right, E2 tell them first, E6 retain counsel — at the prices in the
table above. E3, E4 and E5 are still to come.

**One thing the deck taught us while building it.** The rule "the corner is always the Yes, so
refusing is always free" holds for every temptation, and does not hold for **9**: what was on the
ground when you bought it is yours whether you touch it or not, so there refusing *is* the corner.
That is a second shape, not a mistake, and it is the honest answer to the open question below about
whether refusing should ever cost — it should, when the liability came with the asset. Cards can now
give No actions of their own, and an unanswered card applies them.

## What to build first

Six dilemmas need no new systems beyond the reckoning itself: **22** the survey and **23** the
overheard bid feed the auction · **24** the ministry skips a licence · **9** the old wells are the
deferred abandonment liability · **21** the reserves report moves the credit line · **1** and **2**
use the service hold.

Of the escapes, **E1** and **E2** are the pair that make the mechanic legible. Build those two with
the first cards, and add E3–E6 once the shape is proven.

## Still open

- **How many in a career.** Twenty-eight is a deck, not a schedule. At one every two or three months
  a three-year game sees a dozen.
- **Whether refusing should ever cost.** Every dilemma above is safe to refuse. A deck where virtue
  is always free teaches its own lesson; **11**, **12**, **20** and **24** are the candidates for an
  honest answer that genuinely hurts.
- **What an escape does to the temptation** — settled 2026-09-22. A player may cut corners *planning*
  to clean up. That is a real way of thinking and the game should not pretend otherwise; the answer
  is to price it honestly rather than forbid it.

  The arithmetic says what "honestly" has to mean. Someone playing that way keeps the saving every
  time and pays an escape only when a hint reaches them in time — so if hints arrive in time `h` of
  the time and the corner saved `S`, the plan only loses when `h × E > S`, which needs
  `E > S ÷ h`. At the 60–70% hint rate this deck is aiming for, **an escape has to cost more than
  1.7 times what the corner saved just to break even**, and rather more than that to be a plainly bad
  idea.
